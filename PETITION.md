## I. Introduction and Summary of the Argument

1\. For more than a century, the amateur radio service has served as a proving ground for radio operators, a resource for emergency communications, and a form of goodwill diplomacy between the United States and other nations. A central feature of that service — in the United States and abroad — is the "special event station": a temporary, thematically named call sign that amateur clubs and individuals use to commemorate anniversaries, expositions, disaster-relief operations, and other events of public significance.

2\. Since 1997, the Commission's rules have permitted U.S. amateur licensees to use special event call signs, but *only* in the exceptionally narrow "1x1" format — a single letter (K, N, or W), a single digit, and a single letter (e.g., "W1A").[^1] That 750-combination block was carved out in 1994-95 specifically because it was the *least* commercially useful and thus *least* costly format the Commission could set aside without depleting the sequential and vanity call sign systems used for permanent station licensing.[^2]

3\. In the three decades since, every comparable ITU member administration Petitioner could identify with verifiable primary-source documentation — the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Germany, Ireland, and Japan — has adopted special event call sign systems that permit substantially longer, more flexible, and more thematically expressive suffixes than the United States' 1x1 format, while retaining basic safeguards: issuance by the licensing authority and a limited period of use.[^3] The International Telecommunication Union's own Radio Regulations expressly anticipate and authorize this practice: Article 19, No. 19.68A, added at the 2003 World Radiocommunication Conference, provides that "[o]n special occasions, for temporary use, administrations may authorize use of call signs with more than the four characters" otherwise specified for amateur stations.[^4]

4\. The United States is consequently an outlier. This Petition asks the Commission to exercise the discretion Congress and the ITU Radio Regulations already afford it by amending Part 97 to authorize additional, longer special event call sign formats, administered through the same certified coordinator system that has managed the 1x1 block without incident since 1997. It also asks that those formats be available for amateur radio contest operation, whether or not a contest coincides with a special event. Together, these changes would bring U.S. practice into alignment with the rest of the world.

## II. Statement of Interest of Petitioner

5\. Petitioner is a federally licensed amateur radio operator whose interests, and those of the broader amateur radio community, are directly and adversely affected by the current rule. The scarcity and rigidity of the 1x1 format frequently prevent clubs and event organizers from obtaining a call sign that meaningfully identifies the event being commemorated (for example, a numeral marking a specific anniversary, as the United Kingdom's "Special SES" format now allows), forcing organizers either to accept an arbitrary, non-descriptive 1x1 combination or to forgo a special event call sign altogether. Petitioner has a direct interest in the Commission adopting a rule that permits more expressive, internationally consistent special event call signs.

## III. Background: The Current Regulatory Framework

### A. The Amateur Service Call Sign System Generally

6\. The Commission's amateur radio call sign systems are set out in 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§§&nbsp;97.3 and 97.19, and are administered under the Commission's general licensing authority in the Communications Act of 1934, as amended, 47&nbsp;U.S.C.&nbsp;§§&nbsp;151-155, 301-609.[^5] Amateur stations are ordinarily assigned "sequential" call signs by the Universal Licensing System, or may apply for a "vanity" call sign under 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;97.19, corresponding to formats (informally referred to by the Commission's licensing staff as Groups A through D) tied to the applicant's license class.[^6]

### B. Origin and Scope of the 1x1 Special Event Call Sign System

7\. Separate from the vanity system, 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;97.3(a)(11)(iii) defines a "special event call sign system" under which the station licensee selects a call sign "from a list of call signs shown on a common data base coordinated, maintained and disseminated by the amateur station special event call sign data base coordinators," and that call sign "is substituted for the call sign shown on the station license grant while the station is transmitting." The rule fixes the format precisely: the call sign "must have the single letter prefix K, N or W, followed by a single numeral 0 through 9, followed by a single letter A through W or Y or Z (for example K1A)."[^1] Section 97.119(d) permits a station, "[w]hen transmitting in conjunction with an event of special significance," to identify using this substitute call sign, provided the station also transmits its assigned call sign at least once per hour.[^7]

8\. This system originates in two Commission orders. First, in a Report and Order in PR&nbsp;Docket&nbsp;No.&nbsp;93-305, the Commission reserved the block of "750 one-by-one call signs" — the rarest and least commercially valuable call sign format under the U.S. system — for temporary use during events of special significance to the amateur community.[^2] Second, in *Amendment of Part 97 of the Commission's Rules Concerning Amateur Radio Service*, FCC&nbsp;97-99, WT&nbsp;Docket&nbsp;No.&nbsp;95-57 (adopted March 20, 1997; released April 1, 1997), the Commission codified that reservation into the current rule text and delegated administration of the special event call sign database to Commission-certified volunteer "coordinators" under 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;0.131(p).[^8] The Commission's stated rationale for confining the format to the 1x1 block was one of scarcity: "we do not have additional call signs to assign for this purpose given that any such call signs would have to be taken from the sequential and vanity call sign systems, thus denying them to amateur operators who want them assigned to their primary and club stations."[^9]

9\. That rationale — a zero-sum competition between special event call signs and the finite pool of sequential and vanity call signs — does not apply to the rule change proposed here. As explained in Section VI, *infra*, the formats this Petition proposes would be administered in the same manner as the existing 1x1 block: they would be temporary and coordinator-issued, and they would not draw down the sequential or vanity call sign pools at all.

### C. The Commission Has Already Been Asked to Do This — and Never Ruled on the Request

10\. This is not the first time the idea proposed here has been placed before the Commission. In comments filed March 26, 2010, in WT&nbsp;Docket&nbsp;No.&nbsp;09-209 — a proceeding concerning vanity and club station call sign rules generally — the American Radio Relay League ("ARRL"), the amateur service's principal national association, recommended that the Commission take up, "in a later, separate proceeding, but soon," an amendment to 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;97.3(a)(11)(iii) to expand the special event call sign format beyond the single 1x1 block. ARRL grounded that recommendation in the same 2003 World Radiocommunication Conference amendment to ITU Radio Regulations No. 19.68 discussed in Section IV, *infra*, and observed that "[i]t is time to reexamine this program to determine where deregulatory changes can be implemented to increase flexibility in the program."[^10] The Commission's ensuing Report and Order in that docket, FCC&nbsp;10-189 (released Nov. 8, 2010), addressed vanity call sign reassignment timing and club station license procedures, but did not address, adopt, or deny ARRL's special event call sign suggestion in any respect.[^11] The suggestion appears simply to have gone unaddressed in a docket devoted to other issues, rather than having been considered and rejected on the merits. This Petition formalizes, and requests a ruling on, that sixteen-year-old, previously unaddressed proposal.

11\. A fresh Petition for Rulemaking is the only procedural vehicle now available to obtain that ruling. ARRL's special event call sign suggestion — offered as a passing recommendation for future action within comments filed on an unrelated NPRM — was never itself the subject of a request pending in WT&nbsp;Docket&nbsp;No.&nbsp;09-209 for the Commission to grant or deny. ARRL did separately petition for reconsideration of FCC&nbsp;10-189, but only as to unrelated club-station vanity call sign limits; that petition did not raise the special event call sign format, and the Commission's order resolving it did not address that format either.[^12] In denying ARRL's petition, the Commission expressly terminated WT&nbsp;Docket&nbsp;No.&nbsp;09-209.[^13] Nor is reconsideration available today: a petition for reconsideration must be filed "within 30 days from the date of public notice of the final Commission action," 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;1.106(f), and that window closed in early 2012. With the docket formally terminated and the reconsideration window closed for over a decade, the only avenue by which the Commission can now be asked to act on the special event call sign proposal is a new petition under 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;1.401 — precisely the vehicle Petitioner invokes here.

12\. Petitioner notes, in the interest of candor, that ARRL's reconsideration petition also expressed disagreement with the Commission's earlier decision not to adopt a separate ARRL suggestion to increase the number of call signs available in certain sequential and vanity formats generally. The Commission responded that it had "no plans to revisit the issue at this time," but ARRL did not request reconsideration of that point and the Commission's order did not treat it as one.[^14] That exchange concerned the availability of combinations within the existing, permanent sequential and vanity formats — the same general topic later raised in the 2017 Alessi petition discussed below — and neither the exchange nor the Commission's response addressed or resolved the distinct question presented here: whether the temporary, coordinator-administered special event system should be permitted additional, longer formats.

13\. Petitioner further notes, for the sake of completeness and candor, that the Commission denied one earlier petition for reconsideration of the original special event call sign order: a petition filed by David B. Popkin, denied in *Amendments to the Amateur Service Rules*, Memorandum Opinion and Order, FCC&nbsp;99-84, WT&nbsp;Docket&nbsp;No.&nbsp;95-57, 14&nbsp;FCC&nbsp;Rcd&nbsp;8812 (1999). That petition sought only administrative changes to the special event call sign system — a requirement to identify at the end of a continuous series of transmissions, a fifteen-day maximum duration, a thirty-day interval before reuse of a call sign, codification of first-come-first-served, non-discriminatory, no-charge assignment by the coordinators, and a prohibition on compensation to coordinators for their services. It did not seek, and the Commission's order does not address, any change to the call sign format itself. FCC&nbsp;99-84 accordingly provides no basis to deny, and is not adverse to, the format-expansion proposal made here.[^15]

### D. Neither the 2017 Alessi Petition Nor the 2019 Dukish Petition Is Controlling

14\. Separately, in 2017, Thomas J. Alessi petitioned to make a new "1××1" format (a letter, followed by two digits, followed by a letter) generally available to Amateur Extra Class licensees as an alternative *permanent, sequential/vanity* call sign format, citing a perceived shortage of desirable 1x2 and 2x1 combinations. The Bureau dismissed that petition by letter, citing the roughly 15 million sequential call signs already available and an unspecified, similarly reasoned petition rejected in 2010.[^16] That petition addressed the *permanent* vanity call sign system and a claimed scarcity that the Bureau found factually unsupported — an entirely different rule section and rationale from the one at issue here. It did not address the distinct question presented in this Petition: whether the *temporary*, coordinator-administered special event system, which by definition does not compete with the permanent call sign pool, should be permitted additional, longer formats. Nor does the 2019 denial of Robert Dukish's petition, RM-11826, apply here; that petition sought an identification-timing exception for emergency nets under §&nbsp;97.119(a) and did not concern call sign format.[^17] Petitioner is not aware of any FCC order denying, on the merits, a request to expand the special event call sign format itself. No reported judicial decision addresses amateur call sign format policy; the only governing authority is the Commission's own orders discussed above.

## IV. International Law Confirms the Commission's Discretion to Adopt Longer Special Event Call Signs

15\. The United States' amateur call signs are drawn from international call sign series allocated to it under Appendix 42 to the ITU Radio Regulations (the blocks AAA-ALZ, KAA-KZZ, NAA-NZZ, and WAA-WZZ).[^18] Article 19 of the Radio Regulations governs how those series may be used.[^19] Two provisions are directly relevant.

16\. First, No. 19.38 provides that "[e]ach administration shall choose the call signs from the international series allocated or supplied to it," and expressly excludes call signs assigned to amateur stations from the notification requirements that apply to other services.[^20] The ITU accordingly leaves the assignment of amateur call signs — including special event call signs — entirely to the discretion of the national administration; the Commission need not seek any international clearance to adopt the rule proposed here.

17\. Second, and most directly on point, No. 19.68 sets a baseline format for amateur call signs (a one- or two-character nationality prefix, a single digit, and a group of not more than four characters, the last of which is a letter; No. 19.69 exempts amateur stations from the general bar on using the digits 0 and 1). No. 19.68A, added by the 2003 World Radiocommunication Conference, then creates the specific carve-out this Petition invokes: "On special occasions, for temporary use, administrations may authorize use of call signs with more than the four characters referred to in No. 19.68."[^21] The Radio Regulations — a binding international treaty to which the United States is a party — thus expressly contemplate and authorize exactly the kind of extended, temporary, special-occasion call sign this Petition requests. The Commission's current 1x1-only rule is more restrictive than international law requires; it is a matter of domestic policy choice, not treaty obligation.

19\. Three features of this comparative practice are notable. First, every jurisdiction listed keeps two of the safeguards the Commission already relies on for the 1x1 system: the call sign is issued by the licensing authority, and it is valid only for a limited period. Second, Petitioner has found no report from any of these jurisdictions that longer special event formats have caused interference, database confusion, or misidentification. To the contrary, the United Kingdom's "Special SES" format and Japan's call signs for major national events let the call sign itself communicate the anniversary or event being commemorated — the very flexibility the current U.S. 1x1 format forecloses.

20\. Third, some of these administrations give contest operation its own treatment, separate from special event use generally. Australia charges a separate "contest call sign" reassignment fee (AUD $16) apart from its "special event call sign" reassignment fee (AUD $36).[^28] Canada handles contests case by case: RIC-9 provides that "[c]ertain annual amateur radio contests may be considered as significant events" eligible for a Category IV special call sign, with eligibility "determined in consultation with the Canadian National Amateur Radio Society."[^34] The United States has no comparable contest call sign category or contest-eligibility pathway. U.S. amateurs seeking a distinctive call sign for contest operation instead rely on the club station and vanity call sign systems — the same permanent sequential and vanity pool that this Petition's proposed formats are designed not to draw from, *see* paragraph 9, *supra*. Forming a club for that purpose is an established practice: Part 97 permits a club to hold a club station license through a license trustee and to apply for a vanity call sign for that station.[^35] U.S. contest operators also use the existing 1x1 special event system, but only incidentally: ARRL, one of the Commission-certified special event call sign coordinators, advises that the 1x1 system "[is] not intended for the exclusive ability to have a short call sign during an upcoming amateur radio operating event or contest," although a 1x1 call sign coordinated for a qualifying event "can certainly be used for any Amateur Radio operating purpose during the coordinated time period," including a contest that happens to fall within it.[^36] Petitioner accordingly asks the Commission to make the extended formats proposed in this Petition available for contest operation as well, whether or not a contest coincides with a special event, as discussed in paragraph 25, *infra*.

## VI. The Proposed Rule Serves the Public Interest and Aligns United States Practice With the Rest of the World

21\. The Commission's 1997 order confined special event call signs to the 1x1 block because any additional special event call signs "would have to be taken from the sequential and vanity call sign systems, thus denying them to amateur operators who want them assigned to their primary and club stations."[^9] That scarcity concern does not apply here. This Petition does not ask the Commission to draw special event call signs from the sequential or vanity pools. It asks the Commission to authorize **separately defined, coordinator-administered extended special event formats** with longer, alphanumeric suffixes, analogous to Canada's four-character special event suffixes and Germany's four-to-seven-character suffixes. Those call signs would be issued and tracked by the same certified special event call sign database coordinators that have administered the 1x1 system without reported difficulty for nearly thirty years, and they would be subject to the same hourly identification requirement already codified at 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;97.119(d).

22\. The extended suffix should be permitted to include numerals, not letters alone. ITU Radio Regulations No. 19.68 defines the trailing group of a standard amateur call sign as "not more than four characters, the last of which shall be a letter" — characters, not letters — and No. 19.68A's special-occasion exception removes only the four-character length limit, not that rule on the group's composition.[^21] Other administrations already rely on this to let a special event call sign commemorate the occasion numerically: the United Kingdom's extended "Special SES" format produces call signs such as "GB100XYZ," with the anniversary number in the suffix.[^23] Petitioner therefore proposes that the extended suffix permit any combination of letters and numerals, provided the final character is a letter, as in every other class of amateur call sign. No standard sequential or vanity call sign format (1x2, 2x1, 2x2, 1x3, or 2x3) contains more than one numeral or a suffix of more than three letters. An extended-format call sign therefore cannot duplicate, or be mistaken for, a permanently assigned amateur call sign if it either pairs a single numeral with a suffix of at least four characters or contains two or more numerals, with a suffix of any length. That same safeguard allows the extended formats to use the two-letter prefixes AA through AL, KA through KZ, NA through NZ, and WA through WZ already used in the sequential and vanity systems (the AA through AL block, for example, is reserved for Amateur Extra Class licensees), and not only the single-letter K, N, or W prefix, so long as every call sign with a two-letter prefix contains two or more numerals. The two-letter prefixes substantially enlarge the pool of available special event call signs without giving up that protection.

23\. The extended formats[^37] must also avoid call signs that the Commission's rules and the ITU Radio Regulations reserve or bar for other reasons, and Petitioner proposes four kinds of exclusions. First, format (A) would otherwise reproduce the call signs of broadcast translator stations. A television translator's call sign is the letter K or W, the station's channel number, and two letters (three once the two-letter combinations are exhausted), followed by the suffix -D.[^38] An FM translator's call sign is the letter K or W, the translator's three-digit channel number, and two letters, and an FM translator may identify itself by transmitting that call sign in Morse code.[^39] The Commission already writes television translator channel numbers 2 through 9 as two digits "so as to avoid similarities with call signs assigned to amateur radio stations,"[^38] and the proposed rule respects that policy by excluding any extended-format call sign in either translator format. Second, the proposed rule excludes any call sign in a format assigned to another class of station under the Commission's call sign table.[^40] Apart from the translator formats, no format in that table can coincide with an extended-format call sign: every other format either contains no numeral, ends in a numeral, or places the letter X immediately after its numeral, while every extended-format call sign contains a numeral, ends in a letter, and never has an X after a numeral. The exclusion keeps the rule correct if the table changes. Third, the proposed rule excludes any combination that ITU Radio Regulations No. 19.46 provides "shall not be used as call signs," namely "combinations which might be confused with distress signals or with other signals of a similar nature" and "combinations in Recommendation ITU-R M.1172-0 that are reserved for the abbreviations to be used in the radiocommunication services."[^41]

24\. Fourth, the proposed rule carries over to the extended formats the kinds of call signs the Commission does not assign under the sequential call sign system.[^42] Where that list excludes a prefix only in a particular standard format, such as the 2-by-3 format, the proposed rule applies the exclusion to extended-format call signs with the same prefix and the same suffix length, treating the suffix as the characters that follow the numerals after the prefix. The proposed rule accordingly excludes call signs with the prefix AA through AL, NA through NZ, WC, WK, WM, WR, or WT and a three-character suffix; call signs with the prefix KP, NP, or WP followed by the numeral 0, 6, 7, 8, or 9, at every suffix length, because the list applies that exclusion to every standard format with those prefixes; and call signs beginning with KA2 through KA9, KC4, KC6, KG4, KL9, or KX6. For SOS, the Q-code combinations QRA through QUZ, and the letters EMA that the Commission reserves for Federal Emergency Management Agency stations with the prefixes AF, KF, NF, and WF, the proposed rule goes further than the list, which excludes them only as the entire suffix: it excludes any call sign that contains them, because a longer suffix could otherwise embed them (for example, K10SOSA). The list's remaining entries are already excluded by the extended formats themselves, which never use the prefixes AM through AZ, the letter X in the suffix, or the 1x1 form. With these exclusions, an extended-format call sign cannot duplicate any amateur call sign or any call sign in a format that §&nbsp;2.302 or the broadcast translator rules assign to another class of station. Some format (A) call signs beginning with N, such as N10A, contain the same characters as aircraft registration numbers. Aircraft stations, however, identify by the type of aircraft followed by those characters without the letter N, and existing N-prefix 1x1 and 1x2 amateur call signs such as N1A and N1AB already match registration numbers in the same way, so Petitioner does not propose excluding them.[^43] The pool remains very large. The 1x1 block contains 750 call signs.[^2] Counting only call signs of seven or fewer characters, the extended formats would make 1,325,710,710 call signs available after these exclusions, more than 1.7 million times as many, and call signs of five or fewer characters alone would number 363,000, or more than 480 times the size of the entire 1x1 block (Table 2).[^44]

25\. The extended formats should also be available for amateur radio contest operation, whether or not the contest coincides with an event of special significance. This would not create a separate call sign category. It would be a permitted use of the same extended-format call signs, listed on the same common database, available even when the licensee's only purpose is to operate in a contest. Other administrations already treat contests as occasions warranting a temporary call sign: Australia maintains a distinct contest call sign category,[^28] and Canada allows certain annual contests to qualify for a special call sign.[^34] In the United States, by contrast, a special event call sign may be used in a contest only incidentally, when the contest happens to fall within the dates of a separately qualifying special event.[^36] An operator who wants a distinctive call sign for a contest itself typically must obtain a permanent club or vanity call sign, often through a club formed for that purpose, drawn from the same sequential and vanity pools that the Commission's 1997 order sought to protect. Contest use of the extended formats would not draw on those pools at all because, as shown in paragraphs 22 through 24, *supra*, no extended-format call sign can duplicate a permanently assigned amateur call sign or a call sign in a format assigned to another class of station. Nor does international law stand in the way. No. 19.68A permits longer call signs "[o]n special occasions, for temporary use,"[^4] and nothing in its text confines "special occasions" to commemorative events; a contest with published rules and a fixed operating period is both a special occasion and a temporary use. The same safeguards that govern special event use would apply: an extended-format call sign used for a contest would be entered on the coordinators' common database for the contest's operating period only, and the station would continue to transmit its assigned call sign at least once per hour under 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§&nbsp;97.119(d).

26\. Adopting such a rule would:

<div class="sublist" markdown="1">

a. Bring United States practice into direct alignment with the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Germany, Ireland, and Japan, each of which already permits longer, more descriptive special event call signs administered under comparable safeguards;

b. Exercise, rather than fall short of, the flexibility the ITU Radio Regulations already extend to member administrations under No. 19.68A;

c. Allow U.S. amateur clubs and event organizers to select call signs that meaningfully identify the anniversary, exposition, or commemorated event — for example, a call sign incorporating an anniversary number, as the United Kingdom's "Special SES" format allows — improving public recognition and outreach value of the amateur service;

d. Let U.S. contest operators use a temporary, distinctive call sign for a contest's operating period, even when no special event is involved, without forming a club or taking a permanent call sign out of the sequential and vanity call sign systems; and

e. Require no change to the existing sequential and vanity call sign systems or the existing 1x1 special event system, and require of the certified coordinators only that their existing databases be extended to track the extended formats.

</div>

## VII. Proposed Rule Text

27\. Petitioner proposes that the Commission amend 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§§&nbsp;97.3(a)(11) and 97.119(d) as follows (new text <u>underlined</u>; this is illustrative proposed text for the Commission's consideration and further refinement in a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking):

> **§&nbsp;97.3 Definitions.**
>
> (a) * * *
>
> (11) * * *
>
> (iii) *Special event call sign system.* The call sign is selected by the station licensee from a list of call signs shown on a common data base coordinated, maintained and disseminated by the amateur station special event call sign data base coordinators. The call sign must have the single letter prefix K, N or W, followed by a single numeral 0 through 9, followed by a single letter A through W or Y or Z (for example K1A), <u>or, at the option of the licensee, either of the following extended formats:</u>
>
> <u>(A) The single letter prefix K, N or W, followed by either (i) a single numeral 0 through 9 and four or five characters consisting of any combination of the letters A through W, Y, or Z and the numerals 0 through 9, the last of which shall be a letter (for example K1ANNIV or K100YRS), or (ii) two or more numerals 0 through 9 and one or more characters consisting of any combination of such letters and numerals, the last of which shall be a letter (for example K100A); or</u>
>
> <u>(B) A two-letter prefix AA through AL, KA through KZ, NA through NZ, or WA through WZ, followed by two or more numerals 0 through 9, followed by one or more characters consisting of any combination of the letters A through W, Y, or Z and the numerals 0 through 9, the last of which shall be a letter (for example AA100A or AA100ANNIV).</u>
>
> <u>A call sign in an extended format may not be selected if it:</u>
>
> <u>(1) consists of the letter K or W followed by two numerals and two or three letters, or by three numerals and two letters (the formats of television and FM broadcast translator call signs under §§&nbsp;74.791 and 74.1283 of this chapter);</u>
>
> <u>(2) is in a format assigned to any other class of station under §&nbsp;2.302 of this chapter;</u>
>
> <u>(3) is a combination that shall not be used as a call sign under No. 19.46 of the ITU Radio Regulations;</u>
>
> <u>(4) contains the letters SOS or any of the letter combinations QRA through QUZ;</u>
>
> <u>(5) has the prefix AF, KF, NF, or WF and contains the letters EMA;</u>
>
> <u>(6) has the prefix KP, NP, or WP followed by the numeral 0, 6, 7, 8, or 9;</u>
>
> <u>(7) begins with KA2 through KA9, KC4, KC6, KG4, KL9, or KX6; or</u>
>
> <u>(8) has the prefix AA through AL, NA through NZ, WC, WK, WM, WR, or WT and a suffix of three characters.</u>
>
> <u>For purposes of items (1) through (8), the suffix of an extended-format call sign is the group of characters that follows the numeral or numerals immediately after the prefix. Extended-format call signs are subject to such limits on duration and availability as the Commission may by Public Notice prescribe. A call sign in an extended format may also be used in conjunction with an amateur radio contest, whether or not the contest coincides with an event of special significance, for the operating period of that contest as shown on the common data base.</u>
>
> The special event call sign is substituted for the call sign shown on the station license grant while the station is transmitting. The FCC will issue public announcements detailing the procedures of the special event call sign system.

> **§&nbsp;97.119 Station identification.**
>
> (d) When transmitting in conjunction with an event of special significance <u>or, for a call sign in an extended format authorized under §&nbsp;97.3(a)(11)(iii), an amateur radio contest</u>, a station may substitute for its assigned call sign a special event call sign, <u>in any format authorized under §&nbsp;97.3(a)(11)(iii),</u> as shown for that station for that period of time on the common data base coordinated, maintained and disseminated by the special event call sign data base coordinators. Additionally, the station must transmit its assigned call sign at least once per hour during such transmissions.

28\. Petitioner defers to the Commission and its certified special event call sign coordinators on implementation details, including the precise suffix-length limits, the exclusions described in paragraphs 23 and 24, *supra*, database mechanics, and any transition period. Petitioner likewise defers on how "amateur radio contest" should be defined for this purpose (for example, an operating event with published rules and a fixed operating period) and on whether the same extended-format call sign may be reserved for the same contest in successive years. Petitioner welcomes a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking inviting comment on these questions.

29\. For the foregoing reasons, Petitioner respectfully requests that the Commission institute a rulemaking proceeding to amend 47&nbsp;C.F.R.&nbsp;§§&nbsp;97.3(a)(11)(iii) and 97.119(d) to authorize additional, longer special event call sign formats that may be used for special events and for amateur radio contests, whether or not a contest coincides with a special event, as set forth above, in order to bring United States practice into alignment with the ITU Radio Regulations and with the practice of comparable foreign administrations.
